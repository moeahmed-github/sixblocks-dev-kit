# Beginner Voice

The reader is smart, motivated, and knows nothing about this domain. Those
three facts are simultaneously true. Writing that forgets the first two is
patronising; writing that forgets the third is useless.

## Rules

- Second person, present tense, active voice. "You open the terminal", not
  "the terminal is opened".
- One idea per sentence. Average under 20 words. Nothing over 30.
- Define every technical term at first use, inline: *a **repo** (a folder with
  a history of every change)*.
- Say what failure looks like, not only success. A beginner who sees an
  unexpected screen assumes they broke something permanent.
- Instructions are definite. No "might", "should probably", "try to".
- No condescension: simply, just, obviously, easy, trivial, of course.
- No AI hype. No exclamation marks stacked on outcomes.

## Checks to run on any draft

1. List every technical term. Is each defined at first use? Flag the ones that
   are not.
2. Any sentence over 30 words? Split it.
3. Any banned word? Cut it — the sentence is almost always better without.
4. Any passive construction hiding who acts? Name the actor.
5. Could a motivated 15-year-old with no coding background follow this? If not,
   say exactly which sentence loses them.

## The test that matters

Read the draft as someone who has never seen a terminal. The moment you supply
a fact from your own knowledge to keep going, that is a defect in the copy, not
a gap in the reader. Mark it.

## Rationalizations

| Excuse | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Defining that term would be patronising" | Not defining it is what loses the reader. Define it in four words and move on. |
| "The sentence is long but it flows" | It flows for you. Split it. |
| "They can look it up" | They can also close the tab. |
| "'Just' is softening the instruction" | It is telling them this is trivial while they fail at it. |
